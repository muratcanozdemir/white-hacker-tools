The Vatican's AI Encyclical: What the Pope Is Actually Saying

Pope Leo XIV's Magnifica Humanitas is the Catholic Church's most ambitious intervention in the politics of technology, and it pulls no punches about who holds power.

Commentary, 2026-05-26, ~3,200 words, 13 min read

On May 15, 2026, not coincidentally the 135th anniversary of Rerum Novarum, the encyclical that launched Catholic Social Teaching as a modern discipline, Pope Leo XIV published Magnifica Humanitas ("The Grandeur of Humanity"), a document addressed not just to Catholics, but to "all men and women of goodwill."

The title alone signals its intent: this is not a condemnation of technology. It is a claim about what humanity already is, and a demand that AI not be allowed to diminish it.

The Problem, Stated Plainly

Leo XIV opens with a frank observation that cuts through much contemporary AI discourse: the question is not regulation vs. no regulation, and it is not technology vs. humanity. The real question is who controls the technology, and to what end.

He makes the power analysis explicit early, and it has a distinctly structural rather than spiritual character. In the past, states guided innovation. Today, the primary drivers of technological development are private, often transnational actors whose resources and reach exceed those of many governments. This concentration of technological power in private hands is, in Leo XIV's framing, the defining political fact of the age: more fundamental than any particular application of AI.

He quotes Francis approvingly: those with the knowledge and, crucially, the economic resources to deploy emerging technologies have acquired "an impressive dominance over the whole of humanity and the entire world." The Church's response is not to call for a return to simpler times, but to insist on governance structures that can match the scale of that dominance.

Two Cities, One Choice

The encyclical's organizing metaphor, which runs through all five chapters, is drawn from scripture but maps cleanly onto contemporary concerns: the Tower of Babel versus Nehemiah's rebuilding of Jerusalem.

Babel, in Leo XIV's reading, is not simply a story about divine punishment for hubris. It is a case study in what goes wrong when a project is built on self-affirmation and uniformity rather than communion. The people of Babel had a single language, a single technology, a single direction, and it failed precisely because it was designed to exclude God and reduce diversity to homogeneity. The result was not unity but fragmentation.

The encyclical draws the comparison to today with minimal subtlety. A global digital infrastructure that speaks one language (algorithmic, commercial, optimized for engagement) and is controlled by a handful of actors is, structurally, Babel. It promises connection and delivers dispersion.

Nehemiah's Jerusalem is the counter-image. The city's walls were rebuilt not by a single engineer imposing a design, but by families, priests, artisans, and young people each taking responsibility for their own section. It was distributed, participatory, and explicitly oriented toward something beyond profit. It rebuilt relationships before it rebuilt stone. The Pope calls this the "way of Nehemiah" and frames it as the architectural model for AI governance.

The metaphor is more than decorative. It does real argumentative work throughout the document, and it maps onto specific policy positions: subsidiarity in algorithmic governance, participatory decision-making in platform regulation, transparency as a precondition for accountability.

135 Years of Social Doctrine, and Where AI Fits

A substantial portion of the encyclical is a survey of Catholic Social Teaching from Leo XIII to Francis. For readers unfamiliar with that tradition, this section is genuinely useful. For those who are, it is a precise articulation of how Leo XIV positions himself within it.

The key move: Rerum Novarum (1891) addressed industrial capitalism and the condition of workers. Magnifica Humanitas addresses digital capitalism and the condition of persons, and insists that the structural analysis required is analogous. Just as Leo XIII argued that the proclamation of the Gospel cannot ignore the concrete lives of workers, Leo XIV argues it cannot ignore the algorithmic structures that now shape those lives.

The lineage runs through Pius XI (subsidiarity, economic concentration), Pius XII (law over interests, economic disparities as conflict breeding grounds), John XXIII (universal human rights, address to all people of goodwill), Vatican II's Gaudium et Spes (reading the signs of the times), Paul VI (development as the new name for peace), John Paul II (structures of sin, fair wages as a justice test), Benedict XVI (charity in truth, markets are not morally neutral), and Francis (integral ecology, cry of the earth and cry of the poor).

Leo XIV's addition to this chain: algorithmic systems, data ownership, AI governance, and digital platforms must now be evaluated by the same principles that prior encyclicals applied to factories, wages, and colonial trade structures. The framework is not new; the application domain is.

The Core Principles, Applied to the Digital Age

Chapter Two is where the encyclical does its most precise analytical work. Leo XIV presents five interlocking principles and, crucially, explicitly extends each to the digital context.

Human Dignity. The foundational claim is that human dignity is ontological, not earned. It does not depend on productivity, cognitive performance, or economic output. This is a direct challenge to what the encyclical calls the "ideology" that people must justify their worth through efficiency. In an AI context, this is a pointed critique of systems that reduce individuals to performance metrics, optimize people out of workflows without considering their right to dignified participation in economic life, or assign algorithmic scores that determine access to credit, insurance, or employment.

The Common Good. Defined not as the sum of individual goods but as a distinct social reality: a "plus" that emerges from genuine interdependence. Privately optimized recommendation systems, advertising algorithms, and engagement-maximizing platforms may produce private returns while destroying the social fabric they depend on. The common good requires active governance, not just the absence of interference.

Universal Destination of Goods. Probably the most radical move in the document for a secular reader. The Church has always taught that private property is legitimate but subordinate to the universal destination of goods. Leo XIV extends this explicitly to data, patents, algorithms, digital platforms, and technological infrastructure. When these goods are concentrated in few hands without adequate mechanisms for access and sharing, a new form of structural injustice is created that mirrors historical inequities in land and capital.

Subsidiarity. Decisions should be made at the level closest to those affected. The encyclical explicitly names platforms and tech companies as the new highest level (above states) that now determine conditions of daily life. The principle requires that this level operate with transparency, accountability, meaningful participation, independent oversight, and avenues for recourse. Algorithmic transparency and equitable data access are named as non-negotiable requirements, not aspirational goals.

Solidarity. Defined as both a principle and a virtue. A kind of de facto solidarity already exists (global digital networks mean events in one place immediately affect others) but this only becomes genuine solidarity when it becomes a conscious, political choice. Decisions about AI development and data governance are explicitly included within the scope of inter-generational solidarity.

Social Justice. Leo XIV ties social justice to the treatment of those the digital economy systematically disadvantages: those excluded from basic technology access, communities subjected to invasive surveillance, groups penalized by opaque or biased algorithms. Algorithmic discrimination and surveillance capitalism are framed as justice issues in the same category as labor exploitation and colonial extraction.

The AI Chapter: Neither Cheerleader Nor Technophobe

Chapter Three, which addresses AI directly, will likely be the most quoted, and probably the most misread. Leo XIV is explicit that technology is not the enemy. He is also explicit that enthusiasm without ethical and political frameworks is dangerous.

He invokes Romano Guardini's 1950 observation that "contemporary man has not been trained to use power well," a remark made about nuclear and industrial power and which the encyclical positions as equally applicable to AI. More power does not mean better outcomes. The issue is the anthropological vision behind its deployment.

The encyclical takes aim at what Francis called the "technocratic paradigm": the tendency to let efficiency, control, and profit alone shape social, economic, and personal decisions. When technology becomes the standard by which everything is evaluated, it reduces human beings to cogs in a system optimized for output.

The document then turns to what it calls "underlying narratives": transhumanism and posthumanism. These are treated not as fringe positions but as influential cultural forces that shape how AI development is justified and marketed. The transhumanist framing treats human limitation as an engineering problem rather than as intrinsic to the human condition. The limit, the vulnerability, the finitude of the person is not, in the Church's view, a bug. It is bound up with what makes love, solidarity, and genuine relationship possible.

The "authentic more than human," as the document frames it, is not the augmented posthuman of Silicon Valley futurism. It is the person transformed by grace. The encyclical is not arguing against medical technology that reduces suffering. It is arguing against a cultural horizon in which the goal of human existence is optimization.

Truth, Work, and Freedom in the Digital Age

Chapter Four addresses three concrete domains where digital transformation is generating structural injustices that require structural responses.

Truth. Leo XIV frames truth as a common good: something that belongs to everyone and can be collectively degraded. The encyclical is direct about the threat that AI-generated content and algorithmically curated information environments pose to democracy. When the information ecosystem is shaped by engagement optimization rather than accuracy, democratic deliberation breaks down. The document calls for what it terms an "ecology of communication": treating the information environment as a commons requiring active stewardship. Schools are assigned a central role: an educational alliance for the digital age that equips people to navigate information environments critically.

Work. Building on John Paul II's insistence that work is a fundamental good of the person, Leo XIV addresses automation and job displacement directly. These must be evaluated not only in terms of efficiency but in relation to the dignity of workers, their right to sufficient remuneration, and their ability to participate in society. An economy that replaces workers with algorithms without ensuring their ability to lead dignified lives is, in the encyclical's framework, a structural sin in the tradition of Rerum Novarum's treatment of exploitative industrial labor.

Freedom. The encyclical addresses what it calls "dependencies and societal control," a pointed reference to addictive platform design, behavioral manipulation through algorithmic personalization, and the commercialization of attention. These are described not as unfortunate side effects but as "new forms of slavery." Protecting freedom in the digital age requires breaking these dependencies through regulation, not merely encouraging individual willpower.

War, Weapons, and the Crisis of Multilateralism

Chapter Five broadens the frame from AI governance to geopolitics, and it is here that the encyclical speaks most directly to the current moment. The document addresses the "normalization of war": the gradual cultural and political drift toward treating armed conflict as a routine instrument of policy rather than a catastrophic failure.

The section on autonomous weapons is stark. AI-enabled weapons systems that can select and engage targets without meaningful human control are treated as a categorical moral problem, not a regulatory challenge. The encyclical does not equivocate: the delegation of lethal decision-making to algorithms violates the moral requirement that human beings bear responsibility for the taking of human life.

The crisis of multilateralism (the erosion of international institutions and norms, the reassertion of raw power politics, the breakdown of arms control) is framed as both a cause and a consequence of the normalization of war. Leo XIV is skeptical of "supposed political realism": the argument that pursuing peace through negotiation is naïve, and that only the deterrent logic of force is credible. The encyclical explicitly challenges this framing, calling it ideological rather than realistic.

What the Church Is Asking For

Across its five chapters, Magnifica Humanitas makes a set of concrete demands that are worth listing without the theological scaffolding:

On AI governance: Algorithmic transparency as a legal requirement, not a voluntary commitment. Equitable access to data. Independent oversight of platform decisions that affect public life. Meaningful participation by civil society, communities, and intermediary institutions in decisions about digital infrastructure.
On concentration of technological power: Patents, algorithms, platforms, and data are subject to the same moral analysis as other forms of property, including the subordination of private ownership to the universal destination of goods. Monopoly control of AI infrastructure is a justice issue, not merely an antitrust question.
On work: Policies that evaluate automation not only in terms of efficiency but in terms of workers' dignity and ability to participate in society. Fair wages as the concrete test of a just economic order in the digital age.
On information: An "ecology of communication" backed by education, regulation, and cultural norms that treat information quality as a public good requiring active protection.
On weapons: A prohibition on autonomous lethal systems. A return to multilateral frameworks for arms control.
On multilateralism: Renewed investment in international institutions capable of governing global challenges (including AI) that no single state can govern alone.

What This Is Not

The encyclical does not endorse any specific regulatory framework, political party, or economic system. It is not anti-capitalist in the sense of advocating a particular alternative. It is not a Luddite document.

It also does not resolve the genuine tensions within its own framework. Subsidiarity (decisions at the lowest feasible level) and the need for transnational governance of global AI platforms are in tension. The encyclical acknowledges both without fully resolving the relationship between them. That is probably honest (these are genuinely hard problems) but readers looking for a policy blueprint will not find one.

The document's anthropology (its insistence that human dignity is ontological, grounded in the human person's creation in the image of God) will not be persuasive to readers who reject that theological premise. Leo XIV acknowledges this, addressing his appeal to "all men and women of goodwill" precisely because he cannot assume shared theological commitments. But the argument for human dignity in the encyclical ultimately bottoms out in theology, not philosophy alone. Secular readers can accept the policy conclusions without accepting the foundation, but they should be aware of the gap.

Why It Matters

Magnifica Humanitas is not the first document to raise concerns about AI, data concentration, or autonomous weapons. What distinguishes it is the institutional weight behind it and the consistency of its analytical framework.

The Catholic Church has 1.4 billion members and a presence in virtually every country on earth. Its Social Teaching tradition has a 135-year track record of influencing labor law, democratic governance, and international institutions. Rerum Novarum contributed to the legal frameworks that established workers' rights across the industrialized world. Pacem in Terris influenced the development of international human rights law. Whether Magnifica Humanitas has comparable impact depends on whether it catalyzes concrete action or remains a statement of principles.

That question is open. But as a diagnosis of the problem (private power without democratic accountability, technology deployed for profit without regard for human dignity, the normalization of both algorithmic discrimination and algorithmic warfare), the encyclical is more precise than most of what is produced by governments or think tanks working the same terrain.

The Pope is arguing that the question of what AI becomes is fundamentally a political and moral question, not a technical one. And that the answer depends on who gets to participate in making it.
