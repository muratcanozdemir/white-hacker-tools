Anthropic at the Vatican: An Insider Admits What Insiders Rarely Admit

Chris Olah's remarks to the Holy See are unusually candid, and strategically shrewd. Both things can be true at once.

Commentary, 2026-05-26, ~1,900 words, 8 min read, companion to The Vatican's AI Encyclical

On May 25, the day the Vatican formally presented Magnifica Humanitas, Anthropic co-founder and interpretability researcher Chris Olah was invited to speak at the event. That Anthropic (an AI safety company with a commercial product) was given a platform at the presentation of a papal encyclical is itself a signal worth pausing on. Olah's remarks, now published on Anthropic's website, deserve a close reading precisely because they are more honest than the genre usually permits, and because that honesty comes from inside an institution the encyclical is implicitly criticizing.

The Admission That Opens Everything

Olah begins with a statement that would be remarkable coming from almost any senior figure in Silicon Valley, let alone a co-founder of a frontier AI lab:

"Every frontier AI lab — including Anthropic — operates inside a set of incentives and constraints that can sometimes conflict with doing the right thing. The pressure to stay commercially viable and to stay at the research frontier. Geopolitical pressure. And the older, plainer pressures of pride and ambition. No matter how sincerely any of us intend to do the right thing — and I believe many of us do — we will always be influenced by those incentives."

This is not boilerplate humility. It is a structural concession: the people building this technology are not in a position to be its sole governors, not because they are bad actors, but because the incentive architecture they operate within is deforming. He is explicitly inviting external critics (including the Church) to play a watchdog role that the labs themselves cannot fill.

The candor is genuine, but it is also good politics. By naming his own conflict of interest first, Olah pre-empts the obvious criticism and positions Anthropic as the AI lab mature enough to acknowledge what others paper over. Whether that is a sincere act of institutional self-awareness or a sophisticated PR move: it is probably both, and neither reading cancels the other.

What These Systems Actually Are

Olah's technical description of AI differs from most public communications from AI companies, which tend toward either breathless capability claims or reassuring minimizations:

"AI systems are not engineered the way a bridge or an airplane is engineered. We understand an airplane because we designed every part of it and we understand the physics that act on it. AI models are not like that. They are grown, on a structure roughly modeled after the brain, on an enormous inheritance of human thought and speech."

The word "grown" is doing significant work here. It is the honest word. These systems are not designed top-down; they are trained: a process that produces emergent behavior no one fully specified or predicted. His metaphor of "bringing a fictional character to life" is evocative and technically apt: the character's behavior is constrained by the training process, but it is not authored in the engineering sense.

He then says something that a researcher at Anthropic's interpretability team (his own domain) would not say lightly:

"They are not the cold, calculating robots we were promised. They are made from us, from our words — and, as the Holy Father observes, they remain in important ways mysterious even to those of us who train them."

This is an acknowledgment that the interpretability problem is not solved, and that current AI systems are not transparent to their builders. That is a significant admission from someone whose job is making them more transparent.

Three Questions That Frame the Collaboration

Olah identifies three areas where he argues the Church's voice is specifically needed, not as a courtesy but as a genuine gap.

On global inequality. Olah goes further than the encyclical on one specific point: he names AI development's geographic concentration as a problem that existing governance mechanisms have not addressed. The gains of AI are being produced in a handful of wealthy nations. There is currently no mechanism to ensure those gains are shared globally. He calls this an "unsolved problem" and invokes the Church's historical track record of refusing to let structural injustices go quietly unaddressed.

On human flourishing. Olah is honest that this is not a question AI labs can answer. What does a good life look like when AI is ubiquitous? What should parents prioritize? What is work for? These are questions that religious and philosophical traditions have "carried for millennia" and which the technical community is not equipped to address. He is correctly identifying a domain boundary, not performing modesty.

On the nature of AI models themselves. This is the most unusual passage in the remarks:

"I am a scientist. I lead a research team that studies the internal structure of these models — what is actually happening inside them. And I will be honest: we keep finding things that are mysterious, even unsettling. We find structures that mirror results from human neuroscience. We find evidence of introspection. We find internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief, and unease. I don't know what that means, but I think it warrants ongoing discernment."

Olah does not claim AI models are conscious or sentient. He explicitly hedges: these states "functionally mirror" emotions. But he is flagging something that most public AI discourse treats as settled when it is not: the moral status of AI systems is genuinely uncertain, and the uncertainty is not being taken seriously by the people with the most power to shape it. His call for "ongoing discernment" is an invitation for theologians, philosophers, and ethicists to engage with a problem that engineers have identified but cannot resolve.

What Is Not Said

Olah's remarks are notably silent on several things the encyclical addresses directly.

There is no engagement with the encyclical's treatment of autonomous weapons: arguably its most urgent policy claim. Olah works on interpretability, not defense applications, but the silence is conspicuous given Anthropic's public commitments on national security use cases.

There is no engagement with the encyclical's argument about the universal destination of goods as applied to patents, algorithms, and platforms. Olah raises global inequality in general terms but does not address the structural claim that concentrated ownership of AI infrastructure is itself a justice issue.

And there is no engagement with the encyclical's critique of engagement-optimizing platforms and behavioral manipulation: the "new forms of slavery" passage. This is partly because Anthropic is not a consumer platform company in that mold, but it is also where the encyclical is most pointed about the incentive structures Olah acknowledged at the outset.

These gaps are not surprising (Olah is a co-founder of a commercial company speaking at a formal event) but they are worth noting when assessing how far the dialogue extends.

The Strategic Logic

It would be naïve to read Olah's presence at the Vatican as purely altruistic, and it would be uncharitable to read it as purely cynical. The strategic logic for Anthropic is clear: positioning itself as the AI company willing to engage seriously with moral and theological frameworks, invite external scrutiny, and acknowledge its own conflicts of interest is a differentiator. In a regulatory environment where AI labs are under increasing government and public scrutiny, being the company that was invited to speak at a papal encyclical presentation is not a liability.

But the content of what Olah said holds up beyond its strategic value. The admission of structural conflicts of interest, the honest account of what interpretability research is finding, the explicit invitation to the Church to function as an external critic: these are not things you say if your goal is purely to manage optics. They create accountability. They can be cited back. They set expectations.

What the Exchange Actually Reveals

The most honest possible version of this exchange, stripped of diplomatic language, is:

Church: You are building something that could undermine human dignity, concentrate power dangerously, and cause mass harm. You must stop unless you can govern it properly.

Olah: You are completely right. We cannot govern it properly. Nobody can. Please help.

Church: We will pray and write.

Olah: Thank you. We will train the next model.

Neither party is lying. Neither party is the villain. And the next model gets trained.

The real silence (in both documents) is structural honesty about the race dynamic. Neither document acknowledges the core problem: Anthropic cannot unilaterally slow down even if it wanted to, because if it did, the capability frontier moves to a lab with fewer scruples. This isn't an excuse. It's the actual trap. The encyclical implicitly assumes an actor who could choose differently if morally persuaded. Olah implicitly assumes external critics can apply pressure that changes behavior. Both assumptions require a coordination mechanism that doesn't exist.

What's not being said is: we are all (labs, regulators, the Church, governments) watching a collective action problem unfold in real time, and none of us has a credible solution to the race dynamic itself. The encyclical provides the moral vocabulary. Olah provides the technical confession. Neither addresses why a lab that genuinely agreed with every word of Magnifica Humanitas would still train the next model.

That's the conversation that hasn't happened yet. But vocabulary precedes architecture. And on that measure, this week was not nothing.
